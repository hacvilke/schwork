## 1. What Is Depreciation & Why Must We Record It?

Imagine buying a brand-new delivery van today for **J\$500,000**. After driving it across Jamaica for three years, is it still worth J\$500,000? **Of course not!** Through daily mileage, rust, and newer models entering the market, its value drops steadily.

**Depreciation** is **the systematic allocation (spreading) of the cost of a tangible Fixed Asset as an expense over its estimated useful working life** — reflecting the gradual loss in value of the asset as it helps the business earn revenue.

*(Important facts: **Depreciation is a NON-CASH expense** — no cheque is written to anyone when you record depreciation! And **Freehold Land** is the one Fixed Asset that normally does **NOT** depreciate because land does not wear out!)*

### Three Accounting Principles Behind Depreciation
1. **Accrual & Matching Concept:** Because a delivery van helps earn sales for 5 years, charging 100% of its cost in Year 1 would crush Year 1 profit and inflate Years 2–5. Depreciation matches a fair slice of the asset's cost against each year's revenue!
2. **Prudence (Conservatism) Concept:** Prevents Fixed Assets on the Balance Sheet from being overstated at outdated original purchase prices, and prevents Net Profit from being overstated!
3. **Consistency Concept:** Once a business selects a depreciation method for an asset class (e.g. 20% Reducing Balance for Motor Vehicles), it must apply that **same method every year** so profit figures remain comparable.

### Five Classic Causes of Depreciation (Memorise for Paper 1 & 2!)
1. **Wear and Tear (Physical Deterioration):** Daily use, friction, weather, rust, and decay wearing down vehicles, machinery, and buildings.
2. **Obsolescence:** Faster, newer technology or changing customer tastes make older equipment outdated long before it physically breaks (e.g. older office computers or cash registers).
3. **Passage of Time (Effluxion of Time):** Assets with fixed legal lifespans (such as a 10-year lease or patent) automatically lose value every year even if sitting idle.
4. **Depletion:** Wasting natural assets (bauxite mines, limestone quarries, oil wells, timber forests) lose value as resources are extracted.
5. **Inadequacy:** A growing business outgrows a smaller machine or van that can no longer handle increased demand.

## 2. The Three Factors & Two Syllabus Methods of Depreciation

To calculate depreciation, accountants consider **three core factors**:
1. **Original Historical Cost** *(purchase price + delivery + installation costs)*;
2. **Estimated Useful Working Life** *(how many years the business expects to use the asset)*; and
3. **Estimated Disposal / Scrap (Residual) Value** *(what the asset is expected to sell for at the end of its useful life)*.

<FormulaCard
  title="Depreciation & Net Book Value (NBV) Formulae"
  onFormulaSheet={false}
  summary="Straight-Line charges the exact SAME dollar expense every year; Reducing Balance applies a fixed percentage to the shrinking Net Book Value each year!"
>

<FormulaHint
  block
  label="1. Straight-Line Method (Equal Instalment)"
  short="Subtract Disposal (Scrap) Value from Original Cost and divide evenly by the Useful Life in years — OR multiply Original Cost by the given % rate if stated as '% on cost'!"
>

$$
\text{Annual Depreciation} = \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Disposal (Scrap) Value}}{\text{Estimated Useful Life (Years)}} \quad \text{or} \quad \text{Rate (\%)} \times \text{Original Cost}
$$

</FormulaHint>

<FormulaHint
  block
  label="2. Reducing Balance Method (Diminishing Balance)"
  short="Multiply the fixed % rate by the Net Book Value at the start of the year (Original Cost minus ALL previous depreciation) — so depreciation is largest in Year 1 and smaller each year after!"
>

$$
\text{Annual Depreciation} = \text{Rate (\%)} \times \text{Net Book Value (NBV)}
$$

</FormulaHint>

<FormulaHint
  block
  label="3. Net Book Value (NBV)"
  short="The written-down value of the Fixed Asset shown on the Balance Sheet: Original Cost minus TOTAL Accumulated Provision for Depreciation to date."
>

$$
\text{Net Book Value (NBV)} = \text{Original Cost} - \text{Accumulated Provision for Depreciation}
$$

</FormulaHint>

</FormulaCard>

### Comparing Straight-Line vs Reducing Balance (Paper 2 Theory!)

| Feature | **Straight-Line Method** | **Reducing Balance Method** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Annual Charge** | **Constant (equal)** every year. | **Highest in Year 1**, decreasing steadily each year. |
| **Best Suited For** | Assets that provide steady, uniform benefit with low repair variation (e.g. Buildings, Furniture, Fixtures & Fittings, Leases). | Assets that lose market value rapidly when new AND require higher repair bills as they age (e.g. Motor Vehicles, Computers, Plant & Machinery). |
| **Why Reducing Balance Balances Costs** | — | Early years have **high depreciation + low repairs**; later years have **low depreciation + higher repairs** → total yearly ownership cost stays smooth! |
| **Can NBV Ever Hit J\$0?** | **Yes** (if scrap value = J\$0, hits J\$0 at end of final year). | **Never reaches J\$0** mathematically (always leaves a small residual balance). |

:::warning CRITICAL Examiner Alert (May/June 2025 Subject Report!)
The 2025 Subject Report flagged **TWO huge mistakes** that cost students marks every year:
1. **In the Profit & Loss Account:** Put **ONLY THIS YEAR'S depreciation expense** (e.g. J\$90,000) under Expenses — **NEVER** put `Cost − Depreciation` (e.g. J\$410,000) in the P&L Account!
2. **On the Balance Sheet:** Always subtract the **TOTAL ACCUMULATED Provision for Depreciation** (`Old Provision b/d + This Year's Depreciation`) from Original Cost to show **Net Book Value (NBV)** — NEVER subtract only this year's charge or only last year's opening provision!
:::

## 4. Worked Example 2: Reducing Balance Method (Step-by-Step!)

<WorkedExample
  title="Reducing Balance Method — 20% per Annum for 3 Years"
  marks={6}
  problem={
    <>
      On <strong>1 January 2024</strong>, a bakery buys an industrial oven (Machinery) for <strong>J$200,000</strong>.
      It depreciates machinery at <strong>20% per annum using the Reducing Balance Method</strong>.
      <br />
      Calculate the annual depreciation charge, accumulated provision, and Net Book Value for <strong>2024, 2025, and 2026</strong>.
    </>
  }
  examTip="If a Trial Balance already gives you Machinery Cost J$200,000 AND Provision for Depreciation (1 Jan) J$40,000, ALWAYS subtract the existing Provision FIRST to get NBV (200,000 − 40,000 = J$160,000) BEFORE multiplying by 20%!"
>

- **Year 1 (2024):**
  - Depreciation = $20\% \times 200{,}000 =$ **J\$40,000** | New NBV = $200{,}000 - 40{,}000 =$ **J\$160,000**
- **Year 2 (2025):** *(Take 20% of the NEW reduced NBV J\$160,000!)*
  - Depreciation = $20\% \times 160{,}000 =$ **J\$32,000** | Accumulated Provision = $40{,}000 + 32{,}000 =$ **J\$72,000** | New NBV = **J\$128,000**
- **Year 3 (2026):** *(Take 20% of J\$128,000!)*
  - Depreciation = $20\% \times 128{,}000 =$ **J\$25,600** | Accumulated Provision = $72{,}000 + 25{,}600 =$ **J\$97,600** | New NBV = **J\$102,400** *(Check: $200{,}000 - 97{,}600 = 102{,}400$ ✓)*

| Year | Cost (J\$) | Annual Depreciation to P&L (J\$) | Accumulated Provision (J\$) | Balance Sheet NBV (J\$) |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **2024** | 200,000 | 40,000 | 40,000 | 160,000 |
| **2025** | 200,000 | 32,000 | 72,000 | 128,000 |
| **2026** | 200,000 | 25,600 | 97,600 | **102,400** |

</WorkedExample>
