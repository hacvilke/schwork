## 1. Why Stock Control & Valuation Methods Matter So Much!

Stock (Inventory) is often the **largest Current Asset** in a retail or manufacturing business. Managing stock poorly hurts the business from both directions:
- **Overstocking** ties up cash, raises warehouse/insurance costs, and causes spoilage, obsolescence, or theft.
- **Understocking (Stock-outs)** causes lost sales, angry customers, and idle factory workers waiting for raw materials.

Now consider the **valuation problem**: suppose a hardware store in Kingston buys identical bags of cement three times in March — first at **J\$10**, then supplier prices rise to **J\$12**, and finally **J\$16**. At month-end, **80 identical bags** remain unsold in the storeroom. Which price tag do you assign to those 80 leftover bags?!

**Why your choice matters hugely:** Look back at the Trading Account:
$$\text{Cost of Sales} = \text{Cost of Goods Available} - \textbf{Closing Stock}$$
$$\text{Gross Profit} = \text{Sales} - \text{Cost of Sales}$$
- A **HIGHER Closing Stock** makes **Cost of Sales LOWER** → which makes **Gross Profit & Net Profit HIGHER**!
- A **LOWER Closing Stock** makes **Cost of Sales HIGHER** → which makes **Gross Profit & Net Profit LOWER**!

## 2. The Three Stock Valuation Methods Explained Step by Step

Accountants use **three classic cost-flow assumptions** *(note: these are accounting cost assumptions — they don't always have to match which physical box a clerk grabs off the shelf!)*:

### 1. FIFO — First In, First Out
- **How it works:** Assumes the **OLDEST stock purchased is sold FIRST** (just like milk, bread, or medicine in a supermarket/pharmacy where older expiry dates go to the front!).
- **Which units remain in Closing Stock?** The **NEWEST (most recent)** purchase batches!
- **Step-by-step calculation:** Start from the **BOTTOM (latest date)** of the purchases list and count upward until you reach the exact number of leftover units!
- **Advantage:** Closing Stock on the Balance Sheet reflects **current, up-to-date market replacement prices**!

### 2. LIFO — Last In, First Out
- **How it works:** Assumes the **NEWEST (most recent) stock purchased is sold FIRST** (like a pile of gravel, sand, or bricks where workers scoop off the top of the heap!).
- **Which units remain in Closing Stock?** The **OLDEST (earliest)** purchase batches!
- **Step-by-step calculation:** Start from the **TOP (earliest date / Opening Stock)** of the list and count downward until you reach the exact number of leftover units!
- **Advantage / Disadvantage:** Matches recent purchase costs against recent sales revenue, **BUT** leaves Closing Stock on the Balance Sheet valued at outdated old historical prices!

### 3. AVCO — Weighted Average Cost
- **How it works:** Blends all units available together into **one weighted average cost per unit** (ideal for bulk homogeneous goods like petrol/diesel in tanks, grain, flour, or nails mixed in bins!).

<FormulaCard
  title="Weighted Average Cost (AVCO) Formula"
  onFormulaSheet={false}
  summary="Divide the total dollar cost of ALL goods available by the total number of units available — NEVER just average the unit prices!"
>

<FormulaHint
  block
  label="Weighted Average Cost per Unit"
  short="Always divide Total Cost Available (in J$) by Total Units Available — taking a simple average of prices ignores how many units were bought at each price!"
>

$$
\text{Weighted Average Cost per Unit} = \frac{\text{Total Cost of Goods Available}}{\text{Total Units Available}}
$$

$$
\text{Closing Stock (AVCO)} = \text{Closing Stock Units} \times \text{Weighted Average Cost per Unit}
$$

</FormulaHint>

</FormulaCard>

### International Standard Rule (**IAS 2 — Inventories**) & **Consistency**
- Under **IAS 2** and the **Prudence** concept, Closing Stock on published financial statements is ALWAYS valued at the **LOWER of Cost or Net Realisable Value (NRV)** *(estimated selling price minus any costs needed to repair/sell it)*.
- **IAS 2 permits FIFO and AVCO**, but **DOES NOT PERMIT LIFO** for official published financial statements! *(We still study LIFO in school POA exams so you understand how different valuation methods shift profit!)*
- Under the **Consistency** concept, once a business chooses FIFO or AVCO, it must use that same method every year!

## 3. Effect of Rising Prices (Inflation) vs Falling Prices (Deflation)

Paper 1 and Paper 2 LOVE asking which method gives the highest or lowest Closing Stock and Gross Profit when prices change! Memorise this clean pattern (you never have to guess — just think *"under FIFO, the NEWEST price stays in Closing Stock!"*):

| Market Price Trend | **Highest Closing Stock & Highest Gross Profit** | **Middle Value** | **Lowest Closing Stock & Lowest Gross Profit** |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Rising Prices (Inflation)** *(Most common!)* | **FIFO** *(newest = most expensive stays in Closing Stock!)* | **AVCO** | **LIFO** *(oldest = cheapest stays in Closing Stock!)* |
| **Falling Prices (Deflation)** *(Exact reverse!)* | **LIFO** *(oldest = most expensive stays in Closing Stock!)* | **AVCO** | **FIFO** *(newest = cheapest stays in Closing Stock!)* |

## 4. Worked Example: Same Business Valued All Three Ways (FIFO vs LIFO vs AVCO)

<WorkedExample
  title="Comparing FIFO, LIFO & AVCO During Rising Prices (March 2026)"
  marks={12}
  problem={
    <>
      A Jamaican hardware trader had zero opening stock on 1 March 2026 and made the following purchases:
      <br />
      • March 1: <strong>100 units @ J$10</strong> = J$1,000
      <br />
      • March 10: <strong>150 units @ J$12</strong> = J$1,800
      <br />
      • March 22: <strong>50 units @ J$16</strong> = J$800
      <br />
      During March, <strong>220 units were sold at J$20 each</strong> (Total Sales = <strong>J$4,400</strong>).
      <br />
      Calculate <strong>Closing Stock</strong>, <strong>Cost of Sales</strong>, and <strong>Gross Profit</strong> under (a) FIFO, (b) LIFO, and (c) AVCO, and explain why the three Gross Profits differ.
    </>
  }
  examTip="Before picking FIFO, LIFO, or AVCO, ALWAYS calculate THREE shared totals right at the top: (1) Total Units Available = 300; (2) Total Cost Available = J$3,600; (3) Closing Stock Units = 300 − 220 sold = 80 units left! And NEVER compute AVCO as (10+12+16) ÷ 3!"
>

**Shared Starting Totals:**
- **Total Units Available** = $100 + 150 + 50 =$ **300 units**
- **Total Cost Available** = $1{,}000 + 1{,}800 + 800 =$ **J\$3,600**
- **Closing Stock Units** = $300 - 220\text{ sold} =$ **80 units left** | **Sales Revenue** = **J\$4,400**

### (a) FIFO *(First In, First Out → 80 leftover units come from the BOTTOM / NEWEST purchases!)*
- 50 units from March 22 @ J\$16 = **J\$800**
- 30 units from March 10 @ J\$12 = **J\$360** *(total 50 + 30 = 80 units ✓)*
- **Closing Stock (FIFO)** = $800 + 360 =$ **J\$1,160**
- **Cost of Sales** = $3{,}600 - 1{,}160 =$ **J\$2,440** → **Gross Profit** = $4{,}400 - 2{,}440 =$ **J\$1,960**

### (b) LIFO *(Last In, First Out → 80 leftover units come from the TOP / OLDEST purchases!)*
- 80 units from March 1 @ J\$10 = **J\$800**
- **Closing Stock (LIFO)** = **J\$800**
- **Cost of Sales** = $3{,}600 - 800 =$ **J\$2,800** → **Gross Profit** = $4{,}400 - 2{,}800 =$ **J\$1,600**

### (c) AVCO *(Weighted Average Cost)*
- Weighted Average Cost per unit = $\frac{3{,}600}{300} =$ **J\$12.00 per unit**
- **Closing Stock (AVCO)** = $80 \times 12.00 =$ **J\$960**
- **Cost of Sales** = $3{,}600 - 960 =$ **J\$2,640** → **Gross Profit** = $4{,}400 - 2{,}640 =$ **J\$1,760**

### Side-by-Side Comparison *(Notice the Exact Rising-Price Pattern!)*

| Method | Closing Stock (J\$) | Cost of Sales (J\$) | Gross Profit (J\$) | Key Takeaway |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **FIFO** | **1,160** *(Highest)* | 2,440 *(Lowest)* | **1,960** *(Highest!)* | Highest Asset & Highest Profit during inflation |
| **AVCO** | **960** *(Middle)* | 2,640 *(Middle)* | **1,760** *(Middle)* | Smooths out price swings smoothly in the middle |
| **LIFO** | **800** *(Lowest)* | 2,800 *(Highest)* | **1,600** *(Lowest!)* | Lowest Asset & Lowest Profit during inflation |

</WorkedExample>
