:::info Essential Understanding
Accounting principles exist so financial statements give a **true and fair view** that anyone can trust and compare across years. **Appropriate application** follows rules honestly even when results look modest; **inappropriate application** (often called *creative accounting* or *window dressing*) twists or ignores rules to make a business look richer, more profitable, or less indebted than it really is.
:::

| Quick Focus | What Matters Most |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Key Concept** | Spotting the difference between **appropriate** rule-following and **inappropriate** manipulation ("window dressing") |
| **Exam Focus** | Naming which accounting principle is violated in a case study and showing how profit/assets are distorted |
| **Learning Outcome** | Evaluate accounting treatments critically and correct inappropriate entries back to standard practice |

## 1. Why Principles Must Be Applied Appropriately

Accounting principles (which you will study in full detail in Section 2) are the **shared rules of the game**. When every business follows them faithfully:

- Profit figures are **reliable** (not artificially inflated before a bank loan or deflated before tax filing).
- Statements are **comparable** from one year to the next.
- Assets and liabilities reflect **economic reality** rather than wishful thinking.

When managers deliberately bend those rules to make results look prettier — a practice called **window dressing** or **creative accounting** — readers are misled into bad loans, bad investments, or wrong tax calculations.

### Side-by-Side Comparison: Appropriate vs. Inappropriate Practices

| Accounting Principle | ✓ Appropriate Application | ✕ Inappropriate Application | Distortion Caused |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Business Entity** | Recording the owner’s personal holiday or home rent as **Drawings** (reducing Capital) | Charging the owner’s personal groceries or vacation as a **business expense** | Business expenses **overstated**; Net Profit & taxes **understated** |
| **Prudence (Conservatism)** | Providing for doubtful customer debts and valuing inventory at the **lower** of cost or net realizable value | Ignoring known uncollectible debts or valuing damaged stock at full selling price | Profit and Current Assets **overstated** |
| **Accrual / Matching** | Recording all revenues earned and expenses incurred in the period — including unpaid utility bills | Delaying recording December’s unpaid electricity bill until January just to boost December profit | Expenses & Liabilities **understated**; Profit **overstated** |
| **Consistency** | Using the same depreciation method (e.g. 10% straight line) every year unless genuinely justified and disclosed | Switching depreciation methods back and forth solely to hit a profit bonus target | Year-on-year comparisons become **misleading** |
| **Verifiability (Objectivity)** | Recording every transaction using valid source documents (invoices, receipts, bank slips) | Estimating or inventing expense claims with no receipts or proof | Audit trail broken; risk of **hidden theft or tax fraud** |
| **Revenue Recognition** | Recognizing sales revenue only when goods are delivered or services performed | Counting unsigned customer quotes or advance deposits as completed sales | Revenue and Net Profit **artificially inflated** |

## 4. Worked Example

<WorkedExample
  title="Spotting & Correcting Inappropriate Accounting Treatments"
  marks={9}
  problem={
    <>
      <p>
        <strong>Scenario:</strong> Before applying for a business expansion loan, the owner of
        Crestview Hardware made three adjustments to his draft accounts:
      </p>
      <ol>
        <li>He included his personal family car insurance ($1,400) inside the store’s Motor Expenses.</li>
        <li>He left out $2,200 of December staff wages that were still unpaid at December 31.</li>
        <li>He knew a customer owing $3,500 had gone bankrupt, yet kept the full $3,500 listed as a healthy Trade Receivable so Current Assets looked higher.</li>
      </ol>
      <p>
        For each adjustment: (a) state the <strong>accounting principle violated</strong>,
        (b) state the exact effect on <strong>Net Profit</strong> (overstated or understated and by how much),
        and (c) give the <strong>correct treatment</strong>. <em>[3 marks each = 9 marks]</em>
      </p>
    </>
  }
>

1. **Adjustment 1 — Personal Car Insurance Recorded as Business Expense (3 marks):**
   - *Principle violated:* **Business Entity Concept** *(1 mark)*.
   - *Effect on Net Profit:* Net Profit is **understated by $1,400** (expenses overstated) *(1 mark)*.
   - *Correct treatment:* Remove $1,400 from Motor Expenses and record it as **Drawings** (reducing Capital) *(1 mark)*.

2. **Adjustment 2 — Omitting Unpaid December Wages (3 marks):**
   - *Principle violated:* **Accrual (Matching) Concept** *(1 mark)*.
   - *Effect on Net Profit:* Net Profit is **overstated by $2,200** (expenses and current liabilities understated) *(1 mark)*.
   - *Correct treatment:* Add $2,200 to Wages Expense in the Income Statement and record **$2,200 Accrued Wages** as a Current Liability on the Balance Sheet *(1 mark)*.

3. **Adjustment 3 — Keeping a Bankrupt Customer’s Debt as a Full Asset (3 marks):**
   - *Principle violated:* **Prudence (Conservatism) Concept** *(1 mark)*.
   - *Effect on Net Profit:* Net Profit is **overstated by $3,500** (and Trade Receivables/Current Assets are overstated by $3,500) *(1 mark)*.
   - *Correct treatment:* Write off the $3,500 immediately as a **Bad Debt Expense** in the Income Statement and reduce Trade Receivables by $3,500 *(1 mark)*.

*(Net check: Together, the three inappropriate entries overstated Net Profit by **+$2,200 + $3,500 − $1,400 = +$4,300**!)*

</WorkedExample>

:::tip Quick Exam Checklist
- When analyzing any inappropriate entry, always ask **three questions**:
  1. **Which principle** was broken?
  2. **Which direction** were Profit, Assets, or Liabilities distorted (*overstated* vs. *understated*)?
  3. **What is the exact corrective entry or treatment?**
:::
