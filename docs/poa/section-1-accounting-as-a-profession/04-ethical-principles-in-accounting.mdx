:::info Essential Understanding
Technical skill alone is not enough to be a trusted accountant. If an accountant bends numbers for a friend, leaks private salary sheets, or hides debt to please a boss, **investors, workers, and lenders get harmed**. The International Ethics Standards Board for Accountants (IESBA) Code of Ethics sets **five fundamental principles** that protect public trust.
:::

| Quick Focus | What Matters Most |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Key Concept** | The 5 fundamental ethical principles: **Integrity, Objectivity, Competence & Due Care, Confidentiality, Professional Behaviour** |
| **Exam Focus** | Identifying WHICH ethical principle is breached in a workplace dilemma and recommending the right action |
| **Learning Outcome** | Define each principle accurately and resolve realistic ethical conflicts in accounting |

## 1. The Five Fundamental Ethical Principles

A quick memory aid for all five principles is **I-O-C-C-P**:

| Principle | Clear Meaning | What It Looks Like in Practice | Example of a Breach |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Integrity** | Be straightforward, truthful, and honest in all professional and business relationships | Refusing to sign off on inflated sales figures or fake expense claims | Deliberately backdating an invoice to make this year’s profit look higher |
| **2. Objectivity** | Never allow personal bias, conflicts of interest, or undue pressure from others to override professional judgment | Declining to audit a company owned by your sibling or refusing expensive gifts from a client | Approving a weak loan application because the borrower is a close friend |
| **3. Professional Competence & Due Care** | Maintain up-to-date accounting knowledge and perform work carefully, thoroughly, and on time | Taking continuing professional education (CPE) on new tax laws and double-checking calculations | Preparing complex company tax returns without knowing current tax rules and guessing the numbers |
| **4. Confidentiality** | Respect the privacy of financial information gained through work; never disclose it without authorization or legal duty, and never use it for personal gain | Locking payroll files, using strong passwords, and refusing to gossip about client profits | Telling a rival business about a client’s upcoming merger or buying shares using secret inside data |
| **5. Professional Behaviour** | Comply with all relevant laws and regulations and avoid any conduct that discredits the accounting profession | Filing statutory returns lawfully and advertising services truthfully without disparaging peers | Engaging in tax evasion, false advertising, or harassing colleagues publicly |

:::note When Can Confidentiality Be Legally Overridden?
Confidentiality is NOT absolute. An accountant **may or must disclose** private client data when:
1. The client gives **explicit permission** (e.g. sharing statements with a bank for a loan).
2. **Law or a court order** requires disclosure (e.g. tax audit subpoena or anti-money-laundering reporting).
3. There is a **professional duty** during regulatory quality inspections or legal defense.
:::

## 4. Worked Example

<WorkedExample
  title="Identifying Ethical Breaches & Advising the Right Professional Response"
  marks={9}
  problem={
    <>
      <p>
        <strong>Scenario:</strong> Keisha works as an accounting technician at Starline
        Distributors. Over one month, three situations arise:
      </p>
      <ol>
        <li>Her supervisor tells her to record $25,000 of goods promised for next month as sales in the current year so the branch meets its bonus target.</li>
        <li>At a family dinner, Keisha reveals the exact salaries of Starline’s top managers and mentions that the company is secretly planning to buy a rival distributor.</li>
        <li>A hardware supplier offers Keisha a brand-new smartphone if she ensures his invoices are paid ahead of other suppliers and ignores minor overcharges.</li>
      </ol>
      <p>
        For each situation: (i) state the <strong>main ethical principle violated</strong>,
        (ii) explain <strong>why</strong> it is a breach, and (iii) state <strong>one proper
        action</strong> Keisha should take. <em>[3 marks each = 9 marks]</em>
      </p>
    </>
  }
>

1. **Situation 1 — Inflating Current-Year Sales (3 marks):**
   - *Principle violated:* **Integrity** *(also creates an intimidation threat to Objectivity)* *(1 mark)*.
   - *Why:* Recording uncompleted future sales deliberately falsifies revenue and misleads financial statement users *(1 mark)*.
   - *Proper action:* Politely refuse to record next month's order in the current period, document the facts, and escalate the matter to the Finance Director or Audit Committee if pressured further *(1 mark)*.

2. **Situation 2 — Sharing Private Salaries & Acquisition Plans (3 marks):**
   - *Principle violated:* **Confidentiality** *(1 mark)*.
   - *Why:* Employee payroll details and unannounced merger plans are strictly confidential workplace data disclosed without authorization *(1 mark)*.
   - *Proper action:* Never discuss private employer financial data with family or outsiders, and follow company data-security policies at all times *(1 mark)*.

3. **Situation 3 — Accepting a Smartphone Bribe from a Supplier (3 marks):**
   - *Principle violated:* **Objectivity** *(also Integrity)* *(1 mark)*.
   - *Why:* Accepting a valuable gift creates a self-interest conflict of interest that compromises impartial invoice verification and fair supplier treatment *(1 mark)*.
   - *Proper action:* Decline the smartphone immediately, report the attempted bribe to management, and verify all supplier invoices strictly against purchase orders and delivery notes *(1 mark)*.

</WorkedExample>
